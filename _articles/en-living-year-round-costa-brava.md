There's a real difference between buying a second home to spend July and August on the Costa Brava, and relocating there permanently. Every year we hear from buyers in both situations, and the questions that matter shift quite a bit between the two. This guide is written for anyone considering the second.

## Healthcare: public, private, or both

As a legal resident registered with the relevant system, you have access to Catalonia's public healthcare, with primary care centres in practically every town of any size on the Costa Brava, and referral hospitals in Girona and, for more complex cases, Barcelona.

Many foreign residents also choose private health insurance — for faster access, the comfort of being treated in their own language, or simply out of habit from their home country. The private network in the area is solid, particularly in Girona city and the larger towns along the coast, though in the smaller inland villages private provision is naturally more limited.

## Schools: the question that shapes your choice of area the most

If you're moving with children, the availability of international or bilingual schools is, by some distance, the factor that most limits which towns are genuinely workable for living year-round — not just for summer. International schools are concentrated mainly around Girona city and a handful of larger towns; the smaller, prettier villages of the Costa Brava — precisely the ones that attract the most as a holiday destination — usually only have state or semi-state schools teaching in Catalan and Spanish.

That's not necessarily a drawback: many families see language immersion as a real advantage for their children. But it's a decision worth making consciously, not discovering after you've already bought.

## Connectivity and life outside the season

The Costa Brava changes pace noticeably between July–August and the rest of the year. Many businesses in the most touristy villages — restaurants, shops, even some services — cut their hours or close entirely outside peak season. This plays out very differently depending on the town: Girona city, Palafrugell or Sant Feliu de Guíxols stay active all year; some of the smaller coastal villages go notably quiet between October and May.

For anyone after exactly that kind of calm, it's part of the appeal. For anyone who needs social life and active services twelve months a year, it's worth visiting the area you're considering outside season too, before deciding — the summer photos only tell part of the story.

## Connections to the rest of Europe

Girona-Costa Brava airport offers direct flights to several European destinations, though with a noticeably reduced schedule outside peak season. Barcelona-El Prat airport, roughly an hour and a half from most of the Costa Brava, maintains year-round connectivity to practically any destination. Anyone who travels frequently outside season tends to rely more on Barcelona than Girona for those flights.

## Everyday admin

Registering your address (empadronamiento), your health card, transferring utilities, enrolling children in school, registering as self-employed if you'll be working for yourself — none of these is especially complicated on its own, but resolving all of them at once, newly arrived, without fluent Spanish, is a different matter. A local administrator who understands both Catalan bureaucracy and the typical questions of someone arriving from abroad saves months of unnecessary friction.

## Our recommendation

If your plan is to live here year-round, not just holiday here, the area you choose should be decided with those criteria front and centre — schools, healthcare, life outside season — rather than simply "where would I like to be in August". They're different questions, and sometimes the answer is a different area.
