The purchase price is the figure everyone looks at; the annual cost of owning the home is the one that gets forgotten. There's no single number — it depends on the value, the size, the garden and the pool — but there is a fixed list of items worth adding up before you buy.

## 1. Taxes and charges

- **IBI:** the annual municipal property tax, which depends on the cadastral value and the rate the town hall sets.
- **Municipal charges:** waste collection and, depending on the municipality, other charges.
- **IRNR:** if you're not a tax resident in Spain, you pay tax on an imputed income simply for owning the home, even if you don't let it. We detail it in [taxes and costs for non-residents]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/taxes-buying-property-spain-non-resident/).

## 2. Community fees and maintenance associations

Apartments and developments carry a community fee. In some coastal developments there's also a maintenance association with fees for roads, street lighting and shared areas. Always ask for the recent accounts and minutes, and check whether special levies have been approved or are planned.

## 3. Insurance

Home insurance covers the building and the contents. Many insurers set conditions for homes that stand empty for long periods, such as regular inspections or an alarm: read them before you sign up, not after a claim.

## 4. Utilities

Even when you're away there are fixed charges: electricity (based on the contracted power), water, internet and alarm. Adjusting the contracted power and reviewing the tariffs saves money year after year. Setting everything up on direct debit from a [Spanish bank account]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/spanish-bank-account-non-resident/) avoids missed payments.

## 5. Outdoors: garden and pool

This is the item that surprises people most. Garden, irrigation, pruning, and for the pool the opening and closing of the season, the treatment and the upkeep of the pump. If the house is empty for several months, someone needs to open it, air it and check it before you arrive.

## 6. Maintaining the house

You need to set aside a sum here even when nothing goes wrong: a common rule of thumb is to put aside around 1% of the property's value each year, and more if it's old or has a large pool and garden. A home with a roof or installations near the end of their life needs a bigger reserve, and a [technical due diligence]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/technical-due-diligence-property-explained/) picks that up before you buy.

## 7. Managing it from a distance

Key holding, inspection visits, responding to alerts and coordinating suppliers. If you don't live nearby, it's what brings the most peace of mind and a cost worth budgeting for from the start.

## How to build your budget

Add up the fixed items first (taxes, community fees, insurance, utilities) and then the variable ones (garden, pool, maintenance), and compare them with how much you'll really use the house. If you plan to let it, these costs are the starting point for your net yield, as we explain in [buying to let: real profitability]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/buy-to-let-profitability-catalan-coast/) and in [holiday letting and licensing in Catalonia]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/vacation-rental-catalonia-licence/).
