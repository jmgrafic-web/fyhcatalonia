Among the documents in a property sale in Catalonia there's one that surprises almost every foreign buyer: the habitability certificate (cédula de habitabilidad). It's no minor formality: without it, some purchases get complicated on completion day itself.

## What it is

It's the document certifying that a home meets the minimum habitability conditions set by Catalan regulations: floor area, ventilation, lighting, installations. It's issued by a qualified technician — an architect, a building surveyor or similar — after visiting the property, and it's registered with the regional authorities.

## When it's required and who hands it over

In Catalonia it's generally required to sell or rent a home, and it's the seller who must hand it to the buyer, normally at the notary's office itself. It can also be requested to connect certain utilities. For a new-build home, the equivalent document is the first-occupation certificate, which the developer arranges.

## How long it lasts

The second-occupation certificate, the usual one for a resale property, is currently valid for 15 years; the first-occupation one, for 25. If the certificate on the property you want to buy has expired, it must be renewed before the sale. Always confirm the current validity periods with your lawyer.

## What happens if the property doesn't have one

- **If the home meets the requirements,** it just needs commissioning from a technician and registering: a manageable procedure.
- **If it doesn't** — a room with no ventilation, a floor area below the minimum, an unlegalised extension or an outdated installation — works may be needed before it can be obtained, and sometimes it isn't possible.

Either way, what matters is knowing before you sign the reservation contract. And it's worth putting in writing who deals with it and who pays: by default it falls to the seller, but it's negotiated like any other term of the contract.

## Why it matters more in older homes

Farmhouses and village houses often have decades of renovations and extensions, and they're where a valid certificate is most often missing or where the real condition doesn't match what's documented. We see it often in the [inland Empordà]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/buying-farmhouse-emporda-renovate-guide/), and it's part of what a [technical due diligence]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/technical-due-diligence-property-explained/) checks.

## Habitability certificate, first-occupation licence and energy certificate: not the same thing

- **Habitability certificate:** certifies the home's minimum conditions; registered with the Generalitat.
- **First-occupation licence:** granted by the town hall when a new build or a major renovation is finished.
- **Energy certificate:** rates the property's energy efficiency and is mandatory to sell or let it.

## How to check before you make an offer

1. Ask for a copy of the valid certificate from the first viewing.
2. Have your lawyer verify that it's registered and check its date, something we cover in [the costliest legal mistakes when buying without your own lawyer]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/legal-mistakes-buying-without-lawyer/).
3. Have the technician doing the assessment confirm the home still meets the requirements after any renovations.
4. Agree in writing who arranges it and who pays, and check that it arrives in order for [completion at the notary]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/spanish-notary-completion-day/).
