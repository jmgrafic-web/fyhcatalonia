Every year, hundreds of foreign buyers — British and Dutch buyers especially — fall for some corner of the Costa Brava: a cove in Begur, a cobbled lane in Pals, the light over the water in [Cadaqués]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/cadaques-begur-llafranc-differences/). The desire is simple. Making it happen is a little less so.

Buying a home in Spain from outside the country involves paperwork, timelines and decisions that aren't always intuitive if you don't live here. This guide walks through the real steps of the process, roughly in the order they tend to happen.

## 1. Get your NIE before you need it

The NIE (foreigner identification number) is required for any significant transaction in Spain: buying a property, opening a bank account, setting up utilities, or paying taxes. You can apply at a Spanish consulate in your home country or, once here, at a police station or through a representative with power of attorney.

The most common mistake is leaving it until the last minute. Sorting it out early — before you have an accepted offer — stops a good property slipping away while you wait for an appointment.

## 2. Work out your real budget, not just the asking price

The purchase price is only part of the total cost. When calculating your budget, add transfer tax, notary fees, land registry costs, legal and administrative fees, and, if the property needs it, a [renovation budget]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/renovation-cost-catalan-coast-guide/). On the Costa Brava, the most common purchases sit between €300,000 and €1.2 million, with Begur, Pals, Llafranc and Cadaqués the areas with the strongest international demand.

If renovation is part of the plan, it's worth having a rough cost range from the outset — even an approximate one — so you don't commit to a property that ends up not fitting your real budget.

## 3. Don't buy what you haven't had assessed technically

This is, by far, the stage where most money is lost. A nice listing tells you nothing about the real state of the roof, damp in the walls, the age of the electrics, or whether the last renovation was ever licensed. In older buildings — common in the charming villages of the Costa Brava — these details are the difference between a sound investment and a problem that surfaces six months in.

A technical inspection before signing a reservation contract isn't a luxury: it's the only way to negotiate the price with real data, or to walk away from a property in time. If you want to understand exactly what this step involves, we have a dedicated guide to [technical due diligence]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/technical-due-diligence-property-explained/).

## 4. Understand who's actually working for whom

In a traditional purchase, the estate agent is paid by the seller. That doesn't make them dishonest, but it does mean their natural incentive is to close the sale, not necessarily to protect your interests as the buyer. If you're buying from a distance without your own representation, you often end up coordinating the agency, the notary, the architect and the builder yourself — each with their own schedule and, often, their own language.

Having someone working exclusively for you, with no fee from the seller, changes the whole dynamic of the negotiation.

## 5. Build in time for the reservation contract

Once you've chosen a property, the next step is usually a reservation contract (contrato de arras) that fixes the price and holds the property while the deed is prepared. This document deserves a proper [legal review]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/legal-mistakes-buying-without-lawyer/): penalty clauses, timelines, any charges on the property, and a financing contingency clause if your purchase depends on a mortgage. This is not the moment to improvise with a generic translated template.

## 6. Completion at the notary, and what comes after

The purchase is formalised before a notary, with the public deed as the definitive document. After that come the steps many foreign buyers don't anticipate: registering the change of ownership at the Land Registry, transferring utilities, registering for local property tax (IBI), and, if you won't be living there full-time, fiscal representation as a non-resident. None of these is complicated on its own — managing all of them at once from another country is where it gets difficult.

## The mistakes we see most often

- Viewing only through photos and video, with no in-person technical assessment before committing.
- Underestimating the real cost and timeline of renovating an older building.
- Signing a reservation contract from a generic template, with no legal review tailored to the specific case.
- Not verifying that the property has an occupancy licence, and that any previous renovation was properly legalised.
- Managing half a dozen professionals single-handedly, none of whom know each other or share a common language.
