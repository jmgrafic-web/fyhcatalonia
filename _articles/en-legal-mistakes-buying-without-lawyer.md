"Doesn't the notary already check everything's in order? Do I really need a lawyer too?" It's a fair question, and the answer is no: the notary certifies the act and verifies certain formal aspects, but doesn't act for the buyer against the seller, doesn't negotiate on their behalf, and doesn't necessarily flag everything a buyer would want to know before signing. That's the lawyer's job — and skipping it is, by some distance, the costliest saving we've seen foreign buyers attempt.

## The reservation contract: the first common trap

The reservation contract (contrato de arras) — the document that fixes the price and holds the property before the deed — is where much of a buyer's real protection is decided, and it's also the document most often signed without proper legal review, on the strength of a template supplied by the estate agency. Some of the points a buyer's own lawyer checks, which a generic template rarely protects fairly:

- The exact penalty terms if either buyer or seller pulls out of the deal
- Whether the contract includes a financing contingency clause — critical if your purchase depends on a [mortgage]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/mortgage-financing-foreigners-spain/)
- The real timeline to completion, and what happens if it's missed for reasons outside the buyer's control
- Whether there are any charges, mortgages or encumbrances on the property that need clearing before or at completion

## Charges and title issues nobody tells you about by default

Before signing anything, a lawyer requests an up-to-date Land Registry extract (nota simple) — the document that reveals whether the property carries outstanding mortgages, liens, rights of way or other charges that appear in no listing. It isn't unusual to find properties with a previous mortgage never formally cancelled on the register, even when the seller insists it's already paid off. Without this check, the buyer can inherit a problem that isn't theirs but still affects their property.

## Unregularised extensions and renovations

We touched on this in our guide to [technical due diligence]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/technical-due-diligence-property-explained/): it's not unusual to find past extensions or renovations that were never legalised with the town hall. A lawyer, together with the technical professional carrying out the inspection, checks whether what's actually built matches what's recorded at the Land Registry and in municipal licences — a discrepancy that, if not caught in time, can end in a fine or an obligation to legalise, at the new owner's cost.

## Tax on the transaction, miscalculated

A common mistake is assuming the taxes on a purchase are a single, universal figure, when in reality they vary by autonomous region, property type and the specifics of the transaction. A lawyer or specialist administrator confirms the exact figure that applies to your case before you sign, not after — avoiding the classic unpleasant surprise of an unforeseen cost at the last moment.

## Who actually represents the buyer in a traditional transaction

This is perhaps the point least understood from outside Spain: in a traditional sale, the estate agent works for — and is paid by — the seller. That doesn't make them dishonest, but it does mean their natural incentive is to close the deal, not necessarily to protect every clause in the buyer's interest. Without their own lawyer, the foreign buyer enters the negotiation with nobody working exclusively on their side.

## What it costs, and what it can save

A specialist lawyer's fees for an international purchase are, almost always, a small fraction of the total transaction cost. Compared with the cost of inheriting an undetected charge, an unlegalised extension, or a reservation contract with no financing contingency when the mortgage ultimately falls through, it's one of the best-value items in the whole budget relative to the protection it buys.
