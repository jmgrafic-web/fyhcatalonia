One of the areas that creates the most uncertainty for foreign buyers is tax: what you pay, when, and what obligations remain after completion. This guide covers the main concepts so you arrive at the conversation with your tax advisor with the right questions — it doesn't replace that advice, because exact figures depend on the autonomous region, the type of property and your personal situation, and should always be confirmed with a professional before you sign anything.

## The costs associated with the purchase

Beyond the property price, a purchase in Spain carries a set of costs worth factoring into your budget from the first calculation:

- **Transfer Tax (ITP)** on resale property, or **VAT** if buying new-build directly from a developer. The applicable rate varies by autonomous region — Catalonia has its own rate — and should be confirmed for the specific timing of your purchase, since these rates can change.
- **Notary fees**, for formalising the public deed of sale.
- **Land Registry fees**, for registering the new ownership.
- **Administrative/gestoría fees**, if you delegate filing taxes and paperwork.
- **Lawyer's fees**, if you have [legal review]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/legal-mistakes-buying-without-lawyer/) of the reservation contract and the deed — advisable for any purchase, and especially so if you're buying from abroad.

As a rough reference, these additional costs commonly add up to somewhere between 10% and 13% of the purchase price, though the exact figure depends on the specific transaction and should be confirmed case by case.

## The NIE: the gateway to almost everything else

As we mentioned in our guide to [buying on the Costa Brava]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/buying-property-costa-brava-foreigner-guide/), the foreigner identification number (NIE) is needed to sign the purchase, open a bank account, register utilities and file any tax return afterwards. It isn't a tax procedure in itself, but it's the gateway to everything that follows.

## Taxes that continue after the purchase

Buying the property doesn't close your relationship with the Spanish tax authorities. As a non-resident owner, certain obligations continue for as long as you hold the property:

- **IBI (local property tax)**, municipal and annual, like any owner, resident or not.
- **IRNR (non-resident income tax)**, which taxes an imputed income simply for owning a property in Spain, even if it isn't rented out or generating any income. If you do rent it — as a holiday let, for example — the rental income is taxed as well.
- **Wealth tax**, which applies only above certain value thresholds, with specifics that depend on current regulations.

These obligations are entirely manageable, but they do require a fiscal representative in Spain if you won't be resident there for most of the year — someone to receive correspondence from the tax authorities and file the relevant returns on time.

## If you also plan to let the property

If you're thinking about holiday letting the property for part of the year, there are two further layers worth understanding from the start: the municipal [tourist licence]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/vacation-rental-catalonia-licence/) — whose regulation in Catalonia has shifted over the years and should be checked at the specific time of your purchase — and the specific tax treatment of rental income, which is declared differently from the imputed income for personal use.

## Golden Visa and other residency routes

For buyers from outside the European Union, property investment has historically been one route to Spanish residency through the so-called [Golden Visa]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/golden-visa-spain-requirements/) programme. The regulatory framework for these programmes changes fairly often, so if your interest in buying is tied to obtaining residency, it's essential to confirm the current conditions with an immigration lawyer before making any decision based on it.
