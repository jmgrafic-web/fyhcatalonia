A good number of foreign buyers consider, from the outset, letting their property on the Catalan coast during the periods they're not using it themselves. That's a reasonable expectation — but Catalonia's regulation of short-term holiday lets is among the most specific in Spain, and it's worth understanding before you buy, not after.

## What the holiday-let licence actually is

In Catalonia, letting a property for short-stay tourist use generally requires registering it as a vivienda de uso turístico (HUT) with the regional authorities. It isn't something you resolve by simply signing up to a booking platform: it means meeting requirements for the property itself, registering it formally, and in many municipalities, fitting within a specific quota or urban planning framework.

## The factor that surprises buyers most: the municipal quota

This is the single most important part of the whole framework, and the one foreign buyers most often don't know about: many towns along the Catalan coast — especially high-pressure tourist areas like central Barcelona, [Sitges]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/sitges-buying-guide-cosmopolitan-area/), or several towns on the Costa Brava — limit, or in some cases fully suspend, the granting of new holiday-let licences, depending on current municipal planning policy.

This means a specific property may not qualify for a new licence, even if the previous owner never applied for one. Licence availability depends not only on the property, but on municipal policy at the time — and that policy changes fairly often.

## Why you need to check before you buy, not after

The most costly mistake here is buying on the assumption that "the licence can be sorted out later", only to discover after completion that the municipality isn't accepting new applications in that area or for that type of building. In that scenario, the property is still a great second home — but the rental income plan that part of the budget was built on is no longer viable.

Before buying with letting in mind, it's worth specifically checking:

- Whether the particular municipality currently accepts new holiday-let licence applications
- Whether the property, by its location or type (a flat within a multi-unit building, for example), carries additional restrictions beyond the general municipal rules
- Whether an existing, valid licence is already attached to the property and can be transferred with the sale — in that case, it's usually a genuine added value of the property

## The tax treatment of holiday letting

Holiday rental income is taxed differently from the imputed income on personal use of a second home. As a non-resident, rental income is declared through the [IRNR]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/taxes-buying-property-spain-non-resident/), generally without the expense deductions that resident taxpayers in Spain can claim — a meaningful difference when calculating real net yield, not just gross.

## Managing it remotely: the operational challenge

Beyond the licence and the tax position, holiday letting carries real operational weight: check-in and check-out, cleaning between stays, maintenance, handling issues, and complying with guest registration obligations with the authorities. Managing it remotely, without a trusted local team, is where most foreign owners end up frustrated — not because of the regulations themselves, but because of the day-to-day logistics.
