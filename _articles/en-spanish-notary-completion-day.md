For many foreign buyers, completion day is the most intimidating moment of the whole process: a language they don't speak, a long document and a lot of money at stake. It's far simpler than it looks, as long as you arrive prepared.

## What the notary does (and doesn't do)

The notary gives public authority to the act: they identify the parties, check they have the legal capacity to contract, explain the content of the deed and record how payment is made. What they don't do is negotiate on your behalf or defend your interests against the seller. That's the job of your own lawyer, which we cover in [the costliest legal mistakes when buying without your own lawyer]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/legal-mistakes-buying-without-lawyer/).

## Before completion day

Completion is won in the days before. Three things make the difference:

- **The draft deed.** Ask for it in advance and have your lawyer review it before you sign: it's far easier to fix a detail on paper than on the day, with everyone in the room.
- **The property paperwork.** An up-to-date land registry extract (nota simple) to confirm there are no charges, the energy certificate, the habitability certificate — which in Catalonia is normally required when a home is sold — the property tax (IBI) receipt and a certificate confirming the community charges are paid up. If there's an existing mortgage, it's cancelled at the same signing or before.
- **Your documents and your payments.** A valid passport and NIE. Significant payments are made by bank transfer or bank draft, which the notary identifies and records in the deed. If you're financing the purchase, talk to your bank early: bank drafts need arranging in advance, as we explain in [our guide to mortgages for foreign buyers]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/mortgage-financing-foreigners-spain/).

## On the day

It takes place at the notary's office and, with everything in order, usually lasts under an hour. The notary reads or summarises the deed, answers questions, the parties sign, payments are made and the keys change hands.

If you don't speak Spanish or Catalan fluently, the notary may ask for an interpreter to be present to be sure you understand what you're signing. That's standard practice, and it's worth arranging in advance rather than on the day.

## Signing remotely: the power of attorney

If you can't travel, you can grant a power of attorney to someone you trust — normally your lawyer — to sign on your behalf. It's granted before a notary in your home country, with the relevant apostille and a sworn translation, and must be specific to the transaction. It's worth drafting with your lawyer in Spain so it contains exactly the powers needed, no more and no less.

## After the deed

The purchase doesn't end when you leave the notary's office. Still to come, among other things:

- Payment of the transaction tax (ITP or VAT) within the legal deadline, which in practice the administrator handles. We detail it in [taxes and costs for non-residents]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/taxes-buying-property-spain-non-resident/).
- Registration of the deed at the Land Registry.
- Transferring the utilities and setting up direct debits for the community charges and property tax.

None of it is complicated on its own; what wears people out is handling it all at once from another country.
