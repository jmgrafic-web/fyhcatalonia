Most buyers treat the technical inspection as an optional step — something you do "if there's time", or "if the seller doesn't mind". It's probably the most expensive mistake you can make when buying a home, especially when buying from another country, without first-hand knowledge of local building standards.

## What technical due diligence actually is

It's a full, in-person inspection of the property, carried out by a technical professional — usually an architect or building surveyor — before the buyer commits to any significant payment. At minimum, it covers:

- **Structure**: foundations, load-bearing walls, floor slabs, cracks and their cause.
- **Roof and waterproofing**: damp, leaks, the condition of the waterproofing.
- **Installations**: electrics, plumbing, gas, heating — age and compliance with current standards.
- **Planning and legal status**: whether the building is licensed, whether any previous extension or renovation was legalised, and whether there are any charges or encumbrances on the title.
- **Renovation potential**: what's structurally possible and what isn't, with a cost estimate across different finish levels.

The result is a written report — not a verbal impression given during the viewing itself. That matters: a written report is what lets you negotiate the price with facts, and what protects you if something surfaces later that was already documented before you signed.

## Why a listing is never enough

The photos in a listing show what the seller wants you to see. That's understandable, and perfectly legitimate — but it means the information that actually decides whether a purchase is good or bad is almost never on the property portal: it's behind the wall, under the floor, or in the planning file at the town hall.

In areas with a lot of older housing stock — farmhouses in the Empordà, village houses on the Costa Brava, modernist homes in [Sitges]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/sitges-buying-guide-cosmopolitan-area/) — this matters even more. A renovation that looks "fairly recent" can sit alongside wiring from the 1970s, or an extension that was never legalised and could end up meaning a fine or a demolition order.

## A typical example

Picture a village house with an attractive price for the area. At first glance, it looks to be in good condition. The technical inspection reveals: rising damp on the ground floor from a lack of perimeter waterproofing, an electrical installation with no earth connection, and a second floor built without a licence fifteen years ago.

None of these three findings makes the purchase unviable on its own. But they completely change the negotiation: the price gets adjusted down, the renovation is budgeted with those items included from the start, and a decision gets made about whether it's worth legalising the extension before or after the purchase. Without that report, all three problems would have been discovered — and paid for — after completion, with no room left to negotiate.

## What it costs, and how it pays for itself

Technical due diligence is, almost always, the only step in the buying process with a direct cost to the buyer — the amount varies with the size and complexity of the property. But it's a cost that's easily recovered: if the report lets you negotiate the price down, or steers you away from a purchase that would have gone wrong, the investment pays for itself many times over.

## What to ask before commissioning an inspection

Whether you commission a technical due diligence report with us or with anyone else, it's worth clarifying up front:

1. Is the report delivered in writing, in your language?
2. Does it include a [renovation cost]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/renovation-cost-catalan-coast-guide/) estimate across different levels, or just a diagnosis of the current state?
3. Does the technical professional visit the property in person, or work only from photographs and documents?
4. Does it also cover planning status and licences, or only the physical condition?
5. How many working days does the report take to deliver?

A serious inspection answers all five questions clearly. If any of them raises doubts, it probably isn't thorough enough for a decision of this size.
