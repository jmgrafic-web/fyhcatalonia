A share of the international buyers we work with aren't just after a second home: they want that home to generate income too, when they're not using it. It's a legitimate goal — but the profitability figures circulating on portals and generic articles rarely reflect what an owner actually pockets at the end of the year.

## The gap between gross and net yield

Gross yield — annual rental income divided by purchase price — is the figure most commonly advertised, and the least useful for making a decision. Between that figure and what actually lands in the owner's pocket sits a long list of costs worth subtracting from the start:

- Letting management commission, if you're not managing it yourself
- Cleaning and laundry between stays
- Maintenance and repairs — more frequent in a property with constant guest turnover than in one for personal use
- Community charges, local property tax, insurance
- Periods of the year with no bookings, which on the Catalan coast can be significant outside the May-to-September season
- Tax on the income, without the expense deductions that resident taxpayers can claim

A property advertised at 7% gross can end up, after all these items, at a notably more modest net yield — and that's before accounting for the seasonality specific to the Catalan coast.

## Seasonality: the factor that most distorts the forecasts

Unlike an urban destination with steady year-round demand, the Catalan coast concentrates the vast majority of holiday-let demand between May and September, with a sharp peak in July and August. A profitability forecast that assumes even occupancy across twelve months doesn't reflect the reality of most areas — [Sitges]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/sitges-buying-guide-cosmopolitan-area/) and central Barcelona are a partial exception, with demand spread more evenly through the year.

This doesn't make the investment unviable. It means the correct calculation starts from realistic occupancy concentrated in a handful of months, not an artificial average spread across the whole year.

## The licence: a precondition, not a detail

As we explain in our guide to [holiday letting and licensing in Catalonia]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/vacation-rental-catalonia-licence/), without the correct holiday-let registration, the rental plan simply isn't legal — and in many high-demand municipalities, new licences are limited or suspended. Any yield calculation that doesn't start from having checked this first is, at best, premature.

## Short-term holiday lets versus seasonal lets

Not every letting strategy runs through short-stay platforms. Seasonal letting — stays of several months, common among those working remotely over winter or looking for a long stay without buying — carries a different, generally simpler, tax and management burden than the constant turnover of weekend holiday lets. For an owner who doesn't want to spend time on daily operational management, it can be worth considering, with a lower nightly yield but far more predictable occupancy and management.

## What type of property tends to perform best

In our experience, the properties that best combine rental appeal and value growth aren't necessarily the cheapest, but the ones offering something today's guest consistently values: genuine proximity to the beach or village centre, light and outdoor space, and a standard of renovation that doesn't require explaining to every guest why something doesn't work. An average property in a great location usually outperforms an excellent property in a poor one.
