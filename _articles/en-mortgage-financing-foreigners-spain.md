"Can I get a mortgage in Spain as a foreigner?" is, by some distance, the financial question we hear most after a first property viewing. The short answer is yes. The useful answer has a few nuances worth knowing before you make an offer.

## How much Spanish banks will lend a non-resident

The biggest difference compared with buying as a resident is the loan-to-value ratio. A Spanish resident can typically access financing of up to 80% of the appraised value on their main home. A non-resident buyer, on the other hand, tends to find more conservative terms — generally between 60% and 70% of the appraised value, which isn't always the same figure as the purchase price.

This difference means you need a considerably larger deposit than many buyers initially expect. It's worth budgeting with the more conservative percentage, not the most optimistic figure quoted by any one bank.

## What documentation the bank will ask for

Beyond your NIE, Spanish banks typically ask a non-resident buyer for:

- Proof of income and recent tax returns from your home country
- Bank statements from recent months
- An employment history report or equivalent, and your employment contract if you're salaried
- If self-employed or a business owner, accounts from recent financial years
- A credit report or credit history, depending on the bank

Having this documentation ready and translated before you start the process speeds up approval considerably — and stops a good opportunity cooling off while you gather paperwork.

## Terms and interest rates

The typical maximum term for non-residents tends to sit between 20 and 25 years, shorter than what a resident can usually access, with the added condition that the mortgage normally needs to be repaid before the borrower reaches a certain age — which varies by lender.

On interest rates, non-residents tend to find slightly less competitive terms than residents, on both fixed and variable products. Comparing offers from several lenders — not just the one managing the account where your first payments land — makes a real difference to the total cost of the loan.

## A Spanish mortgage or financing from your home country

Some buyers — particularly from the UK and northern Europe — explore financing the purchase through a mortgage or line of credit secured against a property in their home country, instead of applying for financing from a Spanish bank. This route can offer more familiar, and sometimes more favourable, terms, but it's worth weighing up with a financial adviser who understands both markets — the exchange rate, if the loan currency doesn't match the purchase price currency, adds a variable that doesn't always make up for the saving on the interest rate.

## Insurance tied to the mortgage

It's common for the bank to require home insurance — and sometimes life insurance — linked to the loan, occasionally with better interest rate terms if taken out with the lender itself. It's worth comparing: the "recommended" insurance from the bank isn't always the most competitive on the market, and the price difference can be significant over the life of the loan.
