The inland Empordà isn't the Costa Brava, even though it's fifteen minutes away. It's a different kind of place entirely: eighteenth-century farmhouses, near-untouched medieval villages, vineyards and olive groves as far as you can see, and a stillness the seafront simply can't offer anymore. For anyone after a restoration project with real character, it's one of the few places left in Europe where you can still find that at a reasonable price.

But buying a farmhouse to renovate is a different kind of purchase to buying a move-in-ready home. Here's what matters.

## Who tends to buy a masia

The typical buyer — often British or French — has budget, and more importantly, patience. They're not after the quickest transaction, but the most authentic project: a building with history, its own land, and the chance to shape a home that doesn't exist anywhere on the market already finished.

The key villages are Peralada, Ullastret, Monells, La Bisbal d'Empordà, inland Pals, Cruïlles and Vulpellac. Budget for the property plus a [full renovation]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/renovation-cost-catalan-coast-guide/) typically runs between €200,000 and €500,000, depending on starting condition and size.

## Why so many farmhouses never reach the portals

This is a key quirk of the Empordà market: a significant share of the best opportunities change hands through local knowledge, family inheritance, or a direct sale — never via a property portal. Finding them takes a network on the ground — notaries, administrators, neighbours, other agencies — more than it takes an exhaustive online search.

If your search is limited to what shows up online, you're very likely missing exactly the kind of property you're after.

## The critical points when assessing a masia

A building several centuries old demands a different kind of technical eye than a 1990s flat. A few points deserve particular attention:

- **Load-bearing stone walls**: their condition determines what can be opened up, extended or reconfigured without compromising stability.
- **Roof**: in older buildings, the timber structure often needs partial reinforcement or replacement, not just a new covering.
- **Damp and foundations**: many farmhouses were built with no damp course at all; understanding the source of a stain on a wall matters as much as spotting it.
- **Land registry and planning status**: it's not unusual to find historic extensions — a hayloft turned into a bedroom, an annexe added decades ago — that were never formally regularised.
- **Land and permitted uses**: rural land planning rules determine what can be built or extended, and they vary from one municipality to the next.

None of these points rules out a farmhouse on its own. But they completely change the renovation budget and timeline, which is why it's worth knowing them before committing to a price.

## The real budget: property plus renovation

The most common mistake in this kind of purchase is focusing only on the property's asking price, without setting aside, from the outset, a realistic renovation range. A farmhouse to restore is rarely a "basic" renovation: it usually means a full reconfiguration, updating every installation, and, often, structural work on the roof or floor slabs.

Having a renovation estimate across different levels — essential, considered, premium — before making an offer avoids the most common surprise: falling for a farmhouse whose real restoration cost doubles the original budget.

## Permits and licences: allow for the timeline

Any significant work on a masia requires an architectural project and a council licence, and depending on the municipality and type of land, processing times can run longer than in a built-up urban area. It's worth working with an architect who knows the specific town hall and the type of application involved, not just the general regulations.
