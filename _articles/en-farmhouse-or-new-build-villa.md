It's one of the first, and most defining, decisions a buyer makes when starting to search on the Catalan coast: restore something with history, or build — or buy already built — something entirely new? Neither option is better in the abstract. Each suits a different kind of buyer.

## The farmhouse: character, in exchange for managed uncertainty

A Catalan masia — or an old village house — offers something no new build can replicate: stone walls with centuries of history, proportions and materials nobody builds that way anymore, and an identity that makes the home part of the landscape, not just an object placed on top of it.

The cost isn't only financial. Restoring a farmhouse means working with the uncertainties that come with age: the real condition of the structure isn't fully known until walls are opened up, build timelines tend to run longer than initially budgeted, and the renovation project has to adapt to what the building allows, not the other way round. Choosing this route takes, beyond budget, a certain tolerance for uncertainty, and enjoying the process as much as the final result.

## The new-build villa: predictability, in exchange for character

A new-build villa, or a relatively recent construction in good condition, offers the opposite advantage: a far more predictable budget and timeline, energy efficiency as standard — something a farmhouse rarely achieves without considerable investment — and the ability to move in with no structural surprises left to uncover.

What you give up is precisely that irreplaceable character. A new villa can be beautiful and perfectly built, but it's competing with hundreds of similar homes in the area, while no two farmhouses are ever quite the same.

## Comparing the real budget

It's not correct to assume a farmhouse is automatically cheaper because the starting price for an older building is lower. Depending on its condition, the total cost — property plus [full renovation]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/renovation-cost-catalan-coast-guide/) — of a farmhouse can end up similar to, or even higher than, a comparable new-build villa, especially if unforeseen structural items come up. The real difference isn't so much in the final budget as in how the spend is distributed and, above all, in how much certainty you have about that figure from the start.

## Timelines: the variable that surprises people most

A new-build villa, if already built, lets you move in within weeks of completion. A farmhouse undergoing a full restoration rarely comes in under eight months of work, and projects with significant structural intervention or requiring a major building licence can run considerably longer, mainly because of council processing times rather than the build itself. If you're working to a tight deadline — say, you need to move in by a specific date — this difference should weigh as heavily in the decision as the budget.

## Where each type of property tends to be found

Farmhouses and village houses with the most character are concentrated mainly in the [inland Empordà]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/blog/buying-farmhouse-emporda-renovate-guide/) and some inland parts of the Costa Brava, away from the seafront. New-build or recently built villas turn up more often in developments around Begur, the Maresme, or the area around Sitges, where residential development has been more active in recent decades.

## A question that helps you decide

Beyond budget, the question that really helps you decide is this: do you enjoy the process of creating something as much as the end result, or are you after simply arriving and living, with no more decision-making than choosing the sofa? Neither answer is better than the other — but being honest about it avoids several hundred thousand euros' worth of regret.
